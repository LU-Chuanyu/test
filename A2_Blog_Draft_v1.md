# Why Does Cilantro Taste Like Soap to Some People — and Like Summer to Others?

*The herb that turns dinner tables into debate clubs — and what it quietly tells us about the limits of "blame your genes."*

---

## A herb that splits the dinner table

Order pho at a Vietnamese place with five friends and watch what happens. Three of them dive in. One fishes the green flecks out with practised speed and pushes them to the rim of the bowl. The fifth pulls a face and announces, possibly louder than necessary, that the soup tastes like Palmolive. Cilantro — coriander leaf, if you grew up British — has a way of doing that. Julia Child once said on national television that she would pick it out of a dish and "throw it on the floor" [1]. There is a Facebook group called *I Hate Coriander* with hundreds of thousands of members and an actual manifesto [1]. No other salad garnish has its own hate club.

The interesting question isn't really *who* hates it — it's *why the same leaf, in the same bowl, produces two opposite experiences*. The popular answer you've heard at a dinner party — "oh, it's genetic, you either have the gene or you don't" — is half-true at best. The real story is more flattering to your tongue and more interesting at the table.

## The science in one bite

Most of cilantro's smell comes from a family of molecules called **unsaturated aldehydes** — long, kinked carbon chains with a reactive tip. The same kind of molecule shows up in some soaps and on the back of certain stink-bugs, which is the chemistry behind the famous complaint [1], [2]. When you chew a leaf, those molecules drift up the back of your nose and dock onto **olfactory receptors**, tiny proteins on nerve cells in your nasal lining. Each receptor is shaped to grip a particular kind of molecule. Your brain reads the pattern of which receptors fired, and *that* pattern is what we casually call a "flavour" [3].

*Figure 1. From leaf to verdict in three steps. The same molecule reaches the same receptor in everyone's nose — yet the brain that interprets the signal can land on "citrus" or on "soap" depending on both genetic tuning and a lifetime of meals.*

Two things about that journey matter for the rest of this piece. First, smell is doing most of the work; what we casually call "taste" is mainly an aroma puzzle solved by the brain. Second, the genes that build olfactory receptors are the **largest gene family in the human genome**, with hundreds of versions that quietly differ from one person to the next [3], [7]. That second fact is the door through which both genetics *and* culture walk in to help shape what's in your mouth.

## Perspective 1 — "It's mostly in your genes"

The genetic story has a hero molecule and a hero study. In 2012, Eriksson and colleagues at the consumer-genetics company 23andMe scanned the DNA of nearly 30,000 customers and asked a deceptively simple question: who, on a survey, said cilantro tasted soapy? One genetic variant — a single-letter spelling change in DNA called **rs72921001** — lit up clearly above the noise [2]. It sits right next to a cluster of olfactory-receptor genes on chromosome 11, and one receptor in that cluster, **OR6A2**, happens to grip exactly the kind of aldehydes that dominate cilantro's aroma [2]. A more recent wave of work has put this finding inside a much bigger picture: Trimmer and colleagues showed in 2019 that small genetic differences in the human nose systematically tilt how strong and how pleasant dozens of everyday smells are [3], and Li and colleagues confirmed in 2022 that flipping a single letter in a single receptor can take an odour from "musky and pleasant" to "stinky and unbearable" for the person carrying it [7]. Charles Spence's 2023 review of the whole field still names OR6A2 as the single best-supported molecular fingerprint behind the soap complaint [1].

It is a clean story — a named receptor, a real signal, a huge sample — and at first hearing it feels like a closed case. But the same paper that gave us the gene also quietly gave us a number almost no one repeats at parties: Eriksson's team estimated that genetics, all of it taken together, explains about **8.7%** of the disagreement over cilantro [2]. Not 87. *Eight point seven.* In their replication group, carrying the "soapy" variant nudged people only 0.4 of a point on an 11-point liking scale [2] — statistically real, gastronomically tiny. Newer GWAS work tells the same story at scale: across many odours, single receptor variants usually account for only a few percent of how people experience a smell, with the rest scattered across other genes and, far more importantly, across non-genetic life [3], [7]. The 23andMe sample was also almost entirely European; whether rs72921001 carries the same weight in East Asian, African or Latin-American noses is, as Spence puts it, "still effectively untested at scale" [1]. The gene is real and the receptor is real — but calling *OR6A2* the villain of the story is like blaming the doorbell for who came to the party.

## Perspective 2 — "It's mostly in your kitchen"

The other perspective starts from a fact that is hard to un-see once you've seen it: dislike of cilantro tracks the world's culinary map almost as cleanly as it tracks any gene. In a survey of 1,639 young adults in Toronto, Mauer and El-Sohemy found that about **21% of East Asian, 17% of European-descent and 14% of African-descent participants** said cilantro tasted soapy — versus only **7% of South Asian, 4% of Hispanic and 3% of Middle Eastern participants** [4]. The cuisines at the bottom of that list — Mexican, Indian, Lebanese, Vietnamese, Thai — are the ones that put cilantro in childhood meals almost daily. The cuisines at the top use it rarely or not at all. A 2025 analysis of more than a million online recipes from China, Germany and the United States reached the same conclusion at planetary scale: flavour profiles are local and learned, and they shift within a single generation as immigration and the internet spread new ingredients [5].

![Bar chart: cilantro dislike rates across six ethnocultural groups, ranging from 21% in East Asians down to 3% in Middle Eastern participants](figures/fig1_ethnocultural_prevalence.png)
*Figure 2. A nearly seven-fold gap in dislike rates between Middle Eastern and East Asian diners — a gap no single gene variant has ever come close to explaining. Data: Mauer & El-Sohemy (2012) [4].*

Underneath that map there is a well-established psychological mechanism doing the heavy lifting: the **mere-exposure effect**. Repeated, low-stakes meetings with a food — especially in childhood, especially in pleasant company — quietly move it from "novel" to "neutral" to "preferred" [6]. It is the reason almost no one is born loving black coffee or blue cheese, and the reason cilantro-haters who deliberately cook with it for a few weeks often report that the soapy note recedes and the citrus note steps forward [1]. None of this knocks out the gene story. Mauer and El-Sohemy's ethnocultural categories are blunt and partly mirror genetic ancestry [4]; the recipe-database study tracks what cooks publish, not what diners savour [5]; and while mere-exposure is rock-solid for mild flavours, the evidence that it can fully overwrite a strong soapy percept remains mostly anecdotal [1], [6]. Culture clearly bends the curve. *How far* it can bend a chemically intense aversion is the genuinely open question.

## So which perspective wins?

Lay the two stories side by side and the math, frankly, ends the argument. Genetics — on its own proponents' best estimate — explains under a tenth of why people disagree about cilantro [2], and similarly modest effects turn up across the wider olfactory genome [3], [7]. The other 90% lives in territory that the geographic data describes far better than any SNP: a sevenfold gap between Middle Eastern and East Asian diners [4], echoed in Zhang and colleagues' global recipe atlas [5]. The cleanest way to put the two perspectives together is this: genetics gives you a small **sensitivity dial** — if you carry rs72921001, the aldehydes ring a little louder in your nose [1], [2]. Culture decides what music your brain plays them as, and culture is far more plastic than DNA will ever be [6].

## Conclusion: your tongue is not broken

So here is the takeaway worth carrying out of the restaurant. If you've spent years telling people you "have the cilantro gene," the science is gently asking you to drop the costume. Less than a tenth of your dislike is written in your DNA; the rest is written in the meals you grew up with, and — more importantly — in the meals you have not yet had often enough [2], [4], [5], [6]. That is, in a small way, a hopeful finding. It means a herb you currently can't stand is not a verdict from biology; it is a habit your kitchen can rewrite. And it means the fashionable instinct of our genetic age — to reach for a gene whenever we want to explain a difference between people — overshoots even on a question this small [1], [3], [7]. Cilantro is just a leaf. But the next time someone at the table swears it tastes like soap and shrugs, *"it's genetics, what can you do,"* you can hand them a second taco, smile, and tell them the truth: their tongue isn't broken — it's just under-travelled.
