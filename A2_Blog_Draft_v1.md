# Why Does Cilantro Taste Like Soap to Some People, and Like Summer to Others?

*The herb that turns dinner tables into debate clubs, and what it quietly tells us about the limits of "blame your genes."*

---

## The herb that splits the table

Order pho with five friends and watch what happens. Three dive in. One pushes the green flecks to the rim. The fifth pulls a face and announces, possibly louder than necessary, that the soup tastes like Palmolive. Cilantro has a way of doing that: Julia Child once said on national television that she would pick it out of a dish and "throw it on the floor" [1]; a Facebook group called *I Hate Coriander* has hundreds of thousands of members [1]. No other salad garnish has its own hate club.

The interesting question isn't *who* hates it but *why the same leaf produces two opposite experiences*. Two explanations dominate: a **genetic** account, which says you inherited the variant that turns the leaf soapy or you didn't, and a **cultural** account, which says what you ate growing up trained your nose long before you had an opinion. This blog examines both, and argues that genes do get a vote, but experience casts the louder one.

## The science in one bite

Most of cilantro's smell comes from **unsaturated aldehydes**, which also appear in some soaps and on certain stink-bugs — the chemistry behind the famous complaint [1], [2]. When you chew a leaf, these molecules drift up the back of your nose and dock onto **olfactory receptors**, proteins shaped to grip particular molecules; the brain reads which receptors fired and calls the result a flavour [3]. The two halves of the cilantro debate enter at different stages: the **receptor stage** is where genes vote, since olfactory-receptor genes form the human genome's largest family and their variants differ between people [3], [7]; the **brain-interpretation stage** is where the kitchen votes, because what a firing pattern *means* is learned from a lifetime of meals [3], [6].

## The gene that gets too much credit

The genetic story has a hero study. In 2012, Eriksson and colleagues at 23andMe scanned the DNA of nearly 30,000 customers and asked who said cilantro tasted soapy. One variant, **rs72921001**, sitting next to a cluster of olfactory-receptor genes on chromosome 11, lit up above the noise [2]. One receptor in that cluster, **OR6A2**, happens to grip exactly the aldehydes that dominate cilantro's aroma [2]. Trimmer et al. [3] and Li et al. [7] have since shown that small genetic differences in single olfactory receptors can systematically tilt how strong and pleasant a smell feels, and Spence's 2023 review still names OR6A2 as the best-supported molecular fingerprint behind the soap complaint [1].

On closer examination, however, the genetic account is less decisive than its packaging suggests. Eriksson et al. reported that genetic factors explained only approximately **8.7%** of the observed variation in cilantro aversion [2], which is sufficient to demonstrate an association but insufficient to justify a strongly reductionist reading. The effect size in the replication cohort was similarly limited, with the relevant variant associated with a shift of just 0.4 points on an 11-point liking scale [2] — statistically credible but modest in practical terms. Later large-scale studies of olfactory genetics reinforce this interpretation: single receptor variants can shape aspects of odour perception, yet they typically account for only a small fraction of perceptual variability [3], [7], supporting a model of partial sensory modulation rather than genetic determination. The demographic composition of the 23andMe sample imposes a further limitation on generalisability: given its overwhelmingly European composition, it cannot be assumed that rs72921001 has equivalent salience across other populations, and as Spence observes, this question remains insufficiently tested at scale [1]. The broader supporting literature should likewise be interpreted cautiously. Although Trimmer et al. [3] and Li et al. [7] provide important evidence that olfactory receptor polymorphisms influence smell perception, neither demonstrates that OR6A2 alone can account for the marked cultural and individual variation observed. Overall, the evidence supports a biologically plausible genetic influence, but it does not justify treating OR6A2 as a sole or decisive explanation of cilantro aversion.

## The kitchen that tunes your nose

The other perspective starts from a fact hard to un-see: dislike of cilantro tracks the world's culinary map almost as cleanly as any gene. Mauer and El-Sohemy surveyed 1,639 young adults in Toronto and found that about **21% of East Asian, 17% of European-descent and 14% of African-descent participants** said cilantro tasted soapy, compared with **7% of South Asian, 4% of Hispanic and 3% of Middle Eastern participants** [4]. The cuisines at the bottom of that list — Mexican, Indian, Lebanese, Vietnamese, Thai — put cilantro in childhood meals almost daily. A 2025 analysis of more than a million online recipes reaches the same conclusion at planetary scale [5]. The candidate mechanism is the **mere-exposure effect** [6]: repeated childhood encounters quietly move a food from "novel" to "preferred".

*Figure 2. A nearly seven-fold gap in dislike rates between Middle Eastern and East Asian diners — a gap no single gene variant has come close to explaining. Data: Mauer & El-Sohemy (2012) [4].*

The cultural account, persuasive as it is, also requires careful evaluation rather than uncritical acceptance. Mauer and El-Sohemy's prevalence figures, although striking, derive from a single Toronto sample of young adults who self-reported their dislike on a one-shot questionnaire rather than under controlled tasting [4]. Their ethnocultural categories partly track genetic ancestry, which means the apparent cultural gradient may absorb a portion of the genetic signal it appears to dwarf — a confound the authors acknowledge but cannot disentangle. The 2025 recipe-database study extends the geographic reach of the argument and confirms that flavour profiles are historically mutable [5], yet it measures what cultures *cook*, not what individual diners *enjoy*; published recipes are a reasonable proxy for shared culinary habit but a noisier proxy for personal preference. The supporting psychological mechanism has its own boundaries: the mere-exposure literature reviewed by Byrne [6] establishes that repeated exposure reliably shifts liking for many foods, yet most underlying evidence concerns mild flavours rather than chemically intense, aldehyde-driven aromas, and whether sustained exposure can fully override a strong soapy sensation in genetically susceptible diners remains, as Spence concedes, largely anecdotal [1]. Taken together, these sources offer compelling evidence that cultural exposure shapes the cilantro verdict, but none, individually or jointly, demonstrates that culture *alone* explains the variation observed. The defensible conclusion is calibrated: cultural environment exerts a substantial, well-evidenced influence on cilantro preference, but its magnitude, and its interaction with sensory biology, remain open empirical questions.

## Genes set the dial, culture plays the music

Lay them side by side and the arithmetic is unambiguous. Genetics explains under a tenth of the disagreement [2], with similarly modest effects across the wider olfactory genome [3], [7]; the remainder lives where cultural and exposure data describe far better [4], [5]. Genetics is best understood as a small **sensitivity dial** [1], [2]; the verdict on the resulting loudness is handed down at the dinner table, and that table can be reset within weeks while the dial cannot [6].

## The story we keep buying

Cilantro is the cleanest, smallest version of a story we keep being sold about ourselves: *bad at maths because of my genes, anxious because of my genes*. The claims feel scientific because a real paper attaches a real variant to a sliver of why people differ; they are popular because they close a conversation we'd rather not have. There is even an irony in the cilantro number itself: the 8.7% figure was published by 23andMe [2], whose business model rests on the opposite implication. Same molecule, same receptor, a named gene [2], [7], and still nine-tenths of the disagreement lives in the kitchens that taught us what to find normal [4], [5], [6]. If biology writes only a tenth of the verdict, none of us are simply who our genes were supposed to make.

## Your tongue isn't broken

If you've spent years saying you "have the cilantro gene," the science is asking you to drop the costume. The tongue you grew up with isn't a verdict; it's a draft [2], [4], [5], [6]. So the next time someone waves the green flecks away — *"no thanks, it's just my genes"* — slide a taco across, and ask them to take one honest bite first.
