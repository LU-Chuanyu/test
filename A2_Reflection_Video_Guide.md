# A2 Reflection Video — High-Band Preparation Guide

A working guide for the 3–4 minute screencast reflection on `A2_Blog_Draft_v1.md`
(*Why Does Cilantro Taste Like Soap to Some People, and Like Summer to Others?*).
All section/passage references below point to that draft.

---

## 1. How the reflection video is actually graded

Three things from the LANG 1409 brief that most students miss, and that drive the score:

1. **The video is graded on meta-awareness, not on the blog itself.** The brief says:
   *"show that you understand how your blog post demonstrates the assessed skills … explain why/how each example shows this skill or why you included it in this way."*
   You are not re-explaining cilantro. You are explaining your **writing decisions** against the two assessed criteria: **Critical Analysis** and **Language Use**.
2. **The two assessed criteria are the rubric.** Every minute of the video should be visibly hitting one of them. If a marker can't tell which criterion you're demonstrating at any given moment, you've lost a band.
3. **Form rules are pass/fail-style traps.** No script, no AI voice, no extra visuals, screen-share the blog, 3–4 minutes. Breaking any one of these can cap your grade regardless of content quality.

## 2. Four properties of a high-band video

- **A. Selective, not sequential.** Weak videos read the blog top-to-bottom. Strong videos pick **3–4 specific passages** and justify them. Quality of justification > coverage.
- **B. Names the skill before the example.** *"Here's an example of synthesis across conflicting sources…"* then scroll to it, then explain the choice. This signposting is what tells the marker you understand the rubric.
- **C. Talks about trade-offs and alternatives.** *"I could have led with the gene story, but I chose to open with the dinner-table scene because…"* Showing you considered other choices = critical thinking about your own writing.
- **D. Sounds like a person.** Natural pauses, occasional "uh," self-correction, looking-at-the-screen verbs (*"let me scroll down to…"*). Over-rehearsed delivery reads as scripted and is penalised by the brief.

## 3. Suggested structure (~3:30 total)

| Time        | Section                                  | What you say                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                                          |
|-------------|------------------------------------------|---------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------|
| 0:00–0:20   | Frame                                    | One sentence on the topic, then immediately: *"In this video I'll highlight three places where the post demonstrates critical analysis, and two places where it demonstrates language use for the educated public."* This sentence alone wins marks because it tells the marker exactly what you're about to evidence. |
| 0:20–2:10   | **Critical Analysis** (~1:50, the bigger criterion) | Walk through 3 concrete examples from the blog (see §4 below).                                                                                                                                                                                                                       |
| 2:10–3:10   | **Language Use** (~1:00)                 | Walk through 2 concrete examples (see §5 below).                                                                                                                                                                                                                                      |
| 3:10–3:30   | Close                                    | One sentence on what you'd improve if you had another draft (shows self-critique, signals high band).                                                                                                                                                                                 |

## 4. Concrete moments to highlight — Critical Analysis (pick 3)

> Critical Analysis = synthesis + critique of two perspectives.

- **§3 "The gene that gets too much credit" — the 8.7% number.** This is the strongest single move in the post. Say:
  *"I deliberately put the genetic perspective's own best estimate against itself — Eriksson's paper [2] is the source most often cited *for* the gene story, so quoting its own effect size is the cleanest possible critique."*
  That's textbook *critique using internal evidence*, which is a top-band move.
- **§3 limitation about the European-only sample [1].** Shows you didn't take a finding at face value — you flagged a *generalisability* limit. Name the skill: *"evaluating evidence quality, not just citing it."*
- **§4 vs §3 — the deliberate juxtaposition of the two perspectives.** Point out that the two perspectives chosen aren't symmetric opposites (genetics vs. culture-and-exposure), and explain why that asymmetry is the *point* of the critique: the "debate" is framed as 50/50 in popular media but the evidence isn't.
- **§5 "Genes contribute a little; culture contributes most" — the synthesis paragraph.** Explicitly say:
  *"This is where I synthesize, rather than just summarise — the closing sentence pulls the genetic sensitivity bias from refs [1][2] together with the exposure mechanism from ref [6] into one claim, and that combination is mine, not lifted from any one source."*
  Synthesis is a named rubric word; use it out loud.
- **§4 — the honest concession** (*"Mauer & El-Sohemy's ethnocultural categories partly mirror genetic ancestry"*). Flag this:
  *"Top-band critique acknowledges where your preferred perspective is also weak. I deliberately undercut my own argument here."*

## 5. Concrete moments to highlight — Language Use (pick 2)

> Language Use = engaging, register-appropriate for the educated public, IEEE.

- **The opening pho scene (§1).** Explain the register choice:
  *"The brief says 'engaging, not too formal or informal.' A literature-review opening would be too formal; 'cilantro is yummy lol' would be too informal. A concrete sensory scene with named details (pho, Palmolive, Julia Child) hits the educated-public register."*
- **The 8.7% paragraph's rhythm: "Not 87. *Eight point seven.*"** Point out the deliberate short sentences and italics:
  *"I'm using prose rhythm to make a statistic memorable — that's a science-communication technique, not just style."*
- **IEEE citations integrated mid-sentence, not parked at the end.** Scroll and show `[2]`, `[3]`, `[7]` clustering inside §3. Say:
  *"I use IEEE numerically as required, but I attach citations to the specific claim, not the whole paragraph, so the reader can audit each fact."*
- **Subheadings as argument, not labels.** *"The gene that gets too much credit"* / *"The kitchen that tunes your nose"* — point out that these headings *carry the thesis*, not just announce topic.
- **Figure captions that argue, not describe.** Fig 2's caption (*"a gap no single gene variant has ever come close to explaining"*) — note that the caption itself does critical work.

## 6. Things that quietly cost marks (avoid)

- **Reading the blog aloud.** Markers see this constantly. Always cut to *why I wrote it this way*.
- **Generic praise of your own work** (*"I think this paragraph is really engaging"*). Replace with a *named technique* and a *named rubric criterion*.
- **Going under 3:00 or over 4:00.** Aim for 3:20–3:45 to leave a safety margin.
- **A monotone "list of features."** Vary cadence. Stop, scroll, point with cursor, keep going.
- **Forgetting to mention the *two perspectives* explicitly.** The brief makes this central. Say the words *"the two perspectives I compare are X and Y"* out loud at least once.
- **No self-critique at the end.** One honest *"if I had another draft I'd…"* is a cheap, reliable band-lifter
  (e.g., *"I'd want a non-Western replication of rs72921001 — the post flags it as a gap but doesn't fully resolve it."*).

## 7. Practical recording tips

- **One take, after 2–3 rehearsals of bullet points** (not a script). Use the sticky-note in §9 below.
- **Use the cursor as a pointer** — hover over the exact phrase you're discussing before you talk about it. The screencast equivalent of eye contact; makes evidence concrete for the marker.
- **Open the PDF, not the markdown,** scrolled to the top, zoomed so body text is comfortably readable. Have the References section pre-scrolled-to so the IEEE point lands quickly.
- **Test mic levels with a 10-second sample** before the real take. Bad audio reads as low effort.
- **Don't apologise for "ums."** Natural disfluency is *required* by the brief (*"just talk naturally"*).

## 8. The one-line test before you submit

> *"Could a marker, watching only my video and never reading my blog, write down what 'critical analysis' and 'language use for the educated public' mean to me, and point to where I did each?"*

If yes → high band. If no → re-record the weakest 30 seconds.
